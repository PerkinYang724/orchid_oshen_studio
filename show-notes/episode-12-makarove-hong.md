# Losing Money Is Normal in SaaS: Makarove Hong on 40+ Products, Two-Week MVPs, and Why Decomposition Beats Execution

He ships an MVP in two weeks. He has built more than forty products, and over half of them lost money. He does not describe that as failure — he describes it as the normal ecology of SaaS.

Makarove Hong (洪璿岳) is the founder and CEO of FlyiDEA (心電感應科技), a Taiwanese company that builds no-code and AI products on a two-week delivery cycle, acts as an outsourced CTO for small and mid-sized businesses, and takes a share of the product's margin instead of charging a maintenance fee. There is no fundraising and no IPO clock. In this episode of Still Human, the biweekly podcast from Oshen Studio, Makarove and host Perkin Yang talk about what an AI-run working day actually looks like, why the hard part of the AI era is taking a problem apart rather than doing the work, and what a NT$3,000 internship stipend cost him at nineteen.

## An AI-Run Working Day

The conversation opens on something concrete rather than theoretical: where AI actually sits inside Makarove's day. It shows up in the first second the alarm goes off, and it keeps working in places most people would not think to put it — including agents that watch multiple LINE groups for messages that carry risk, so that the signal surfaces without him reading every thread.

## Product Hunt, Every Day, Fed to a Machine

He reads Product Hunt daily. The interesting part is not the reading; it is what happens next. The products go to an AI that pulls them apart — structure, positioning, what the thing is actually doing underneath the landing page. It is a habit of teardown rather than consumption, and it feeds directly into how quickly his team can rebuild an idea.

## The Two-Week MVP, and What He Still Touches

FlyiDEA delivers an MVP in two weeks. Makarove walks through the fourteen days honestly: which parts are no-code, which parts are AI, and how much he still builds by hand between day one and day fourteen. The answer is less flattering to the tooling than the pitch usually is, and more useful.

## RiiCH, Profit Share, and Losing Money on Half of Everything

FlyiDEA takes a share of margin rather than a maintenance fee — a model that ties the company's outcome to whether the product actually works. Makarove is blunt about the arithmetic: across more than forty products, over half lost money. RiiCH is one of the products in that portfolio. His position is that this distribution is not a sign of doing it wrong; it is what the SaaS ecology looks like from the inside, and pretending otherwise is how founders misread their own numbers.

## Two Years, No Launch, No Revenue

The counterweight to the two-week story is a project that ran for two years, never went live, and never earned a dollar. He tells it without softening it. It is the clearest illustration in the episode of what happens when the decomposition is wrong at the start.

## Decomposition Is the Bottleneck

This is the argument at the center of the episode. Execution has gotten cheap — models write the code, no-code covers the scaffolding, and a two-week MVP is possible because of it. What has not gotten cheap is knowing how to cut a problem into the right pieces before any of that starts. Makarove's claim is that the scarce skill in the AI era is decomposition, and that most teams are still optimizing the part that stopped being hard.

## Growth Happens at the Limit

A shift in register: he argues that growth does not happen in loss, it happens at the limit — and that when what is behind you is frightening enough, moving forward stops being a decision. It is the frame he uses to explain the next part.

## Taipei 101, Three Hours of Sleep, and the Activation Phase

At university, an internship paid a NT$3,000 monthly stipend. He needed NT$11,000 to live — rent 4,000, food 6,000, gas 1,000 — so he took night shifts at Taipei 101 to cover the other NT$8,000, sleeping three hours a day. Two weeks in, he fell down the stairs. He does not tell the story as a grind anecdote or a warning; he calls that stretch the activation phase, and the episode sits with what that framing does and does not justify.

## Culture Over Salary

On building a team, he puts culture above pay and breaks it into three things he looks for: ability, willingness, and emotional connection. He also describes treating himself as a black hole — absorbing pressure so it does not land on the people around him — which is either a leadership philosophy or a load-bearing wall, and the conversation does not fully resolve which.

## Ten Minutes That Are Not Optimized

Asked for the most relaxed stretch of his week, the answer is not a system: walking home for an hour, looking at the moon. For someone who has automated the first second of his morning, it is the part of the week he has deliberately left alone.
